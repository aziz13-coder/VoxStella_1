# Worst Roommate Ever Cases Through Vox Stella's Forensic Astrology Engine

Netflix's `Worst Roommate Ever` is built around a fear most people understand immediately: the person sharing your home may know too much, see too much, and cross too many boundaries before anyone outside the house realizes what is happening.

That makes the series a useful stress test for forensic astrology.

Not because astrology can replace police work. It cannot. Not because a chart can prove who committed a crime. It cannot do that either. The value of a forensic astrology tool is more modest and more practical: when we give it a documented event time and place, does it surface the same broad case pattern that later became clear through investigation, court records, or official reporting?

Vox Stella recently tested that question against the real cases shown in `Worst Roommate Ever`. The goal was not to dramatize the cases. The goal was to see whether the forensic engine could behave responsibly against source-backed true-crime material.

The answer was interesting: the engine performed best when the public record gave a tight event anchor, and it became less reliable when the only available timing was broad, approximate, or reconstructed after the fact.

That is exactly the kind of distinction forensic astrology needs to make.

## How we selected the three cases

We inventoried all nine released episodes across the two released seasons of `Worst Roommate Ever` as of May 13, 2026. Netflix/Tudum was used to confirm the episode inventory, while case facts were checked against court records, police releases, prosecutor records, and reputable reporting where available.

Only four cases were runnable in the benchmark. A case had to have a public date, location, and documented time or tight response-time anchor. We did not invent noon charts, guess death times, or use birth charts.

The four runnable anchors were:

| Case | Public anchor used | Benchmark result |
|---|---|---|
| Maribel Ramos / K.C. Joy | Last seen around 8:30 p.m. in Orange, California | Primary pattern aligned |
| Harry Bachman / Jamison Bachman | Welfare-check discovery dispatch in Elkins Park, Pennsylvania | Partially aligned |
| Anita Cowen / Scott Pettigrew | Police welfare-check dispatch in Cathedral City, California | Primary pattern aligned |
| Jessica Lewis and Austin Wenner / Michael Dudley | Probable evening sequence in Burien, Washington | Primary pattern aligned, survivability undercalled |

Five other cases stayed in the inventory but were held out of live scoring because the public source set did not provide a precise enough event time: Dorothea Puente, Youssef Khater and Callie Quinn, Jamison Bachman Part 1, Janie Ridd and Rachel, and Tammy Fritz and James "Bo" Bowden.

That leaves three headline examples for this post. They are not the "best" cases in a dramatic sense. They are the cases where the engine had enough timing structure to make a fair run and aligned with the primary case pattern.

## Case 1: Maribel Ramos and K.C. Joy

Episode search intent: `Be Careful of the Quiet Ones true story`, `Worst Roommate Ever Maribel Ramos`, `K.C. Joy Maribel Ramos`

The Netflix episode `Be Careful of the Quiet Ones` focuses on Maribel Ramos, an Iraq War veteran and Cal State Fullerton student, and her roommate Kwang Chol "K.C." Joy.

The event anchor used in Vox Stella was not an exact death time. It was the public last-seen report: Ramos was last seen outside her Orange, California apartment around 8:30 p.m. on May 2, 2013. ABC7 reported that last-seen timing while Ramos was still missing. CBS Los Angeles later reported that Joy was convicted of second-degree murder and sentenced to 15 years to life.

That distinction matters. The chart is a disappearance or last-contact chart, not a certified homicide chart.

### What Vox Stella matched

The forensic engine aligned on the three main case directions:

- violence or homicide pressure
- missing-person or disappearance pressure
- a roommate, friend, or known-associate axis

It also returned a lower survivability reading, which matched the fatal outcome later established by the case.

In practical terms, this was one of the cleaner reads in the benchmark. The chart did not need to know the full documentary narrative to flag the core shape: a missing-person case that later resolved as a homicide involving a known person in the home environment.

### What to be careful about

The engine also surfaced broader family, water, and witness-style categories. In a forensic astrology workflow, those should be treated as exploratory context, not as factual claims.

That is an important product lesson. A strong forensic reading is not the same thing as a full reconstruction. Vox Stella can show useful pressure areas, but the user still has to keep the source record separate from the symbolic read.

### How a user would enter this case

Use manual event mode and label the chart as a last-seen anchor:

- Date: May 2, 2013
- Time: 8:30 p.m.
- Location: Orange, California
- Time zone: America/Los_Angeles
- Anchor type: last seen / disappearance

The right question is not "does this chart prove what happened?" The better question is: "does this chart identify the same broad risk pattern that the known case later confirmed?"

For Ramos, the answer was yes.

This is exactly the kind of case where forensic astrology has a fairer chance to behave consistently: tight date, tight location, public dispatch time, and a documented relationship context.

Again, the chart does not prove the fact. But in this case, the symbolic category and the source-backed case feature pointed in the same direction.

The Cowen/Pettigrew case shows the forensic feature at its most useful:

- The source anchor is close to the emergency response.
- The relationship context is explicit: lodger, roommate, shared home.

## Case 3: Jessica Lewis, Austin Wenner, and Michael Dudley

Episode search intent: `The Lethal Landlord true story`, `Michael Dudley Jessica Lewis Austin Wenner`, `Michael Dudley suitcase murders`

`The Lethal Landlord` centers on Michael Lee Dudley and the deaths of Jessica Lewis and Austin Wenner, who had been staying or renting in Dudley's Burien, Washington home.

This case is one of the most important examples in the benchmark because it produced both a hit and a visible limitation.

The Washington Court of Appeals opinion describes Dudley's convictions for two counts of second-degree murder while armed with a firearm and discusses the discovery of remains near Seattle water sites. FOX 13 Seattle reported that Dudley was sentenced to 560 months, or more than 46 years, and that the King County Medical Examiner determined Lewis and Wenner died on or about June 9, 2020.

For the benchmark, Vox Stella used a probable evening sequence anchor from charging-document reporting: June 9, 2020 around 7:00 p.m. in Burien. That is not an exact time of death. It is a proxy sequence anchor, and it has to be treated as lower-confidence than the Cowen dispatch time.

### What Vox Stella matched

The engine aligned on the primary case pattern:

- homicide or fatal violence
- known-associate, tenant, or roommate-style proximity
- deception, concealment, or cover-up pressure

It also surfaced water-related pressure, which fits the later disposal and discovery context near Seattle water sites.

That is the useful side of the result. The engine saw the case as violence involving known people in a housing relationship, with concealment as a major part of the pattern.

### Where Vox Stella missed

The survivability layer did not fully align. Instead of returning a lower fatality band, the engine returned a moderate, risk-loaded survival band.

That miss should stay in the article.

It is tempting to hide it because the primary case pattern aligned, but the miss is exactly what makes the benchmark credible. Vox Stella did not simply turn a Netflix case into a polished success story. It preserved the gap: when the event time is a probable sequence anchor rather than an exact death or response time, survivability can become less reliable.

That is a useful warning for real forensic astrology work.

## What the three cases teach about forensic astrology

These three `Worst Roommate Ever` cases point to a few practical rules for using forensic astrology software responsibly.

First, event timing matters more than the drama of the case.

The most emotionally intense cases are not always the best chart examples. A case with a tight dispatch time may be more useful than a famous case with only a broad date range. That is why Dorothea Puente, Janie Ridd, Tammy Fritz, Youssef Khater, and parts of the Jamison Bachman story stayed in the inventory but were not used as headline engine examples.

Second, relationship context matters.

The strongest alignments came from cases where the relationship category was clear: roommate, lodger, tenant, landlord, known person in the home. Vox Stella's forensic workflow is strongest when the user labels the real-world context carefully instead of treating every event chart as a generic crime chart.

Third, disappearance, homicide, and concealment can overlap.

The Ramos case began publicly as a missing-person case but later resolved as a homicide conviction. The Dudley case involved homicide plus concealment and later water-site discoveries. A good forensic workflow needs to show overlapping categories without pretending every category is equally central.

Fourth, misses should be preserved.

The Dudley survivability miss is not a reason to discard the feature. It is a reason to use it more carefully. A transparent benchmark is more persuasive than a perfect-looking demonstration with the hard parts edited out.

## Suggested FAQ

### Is forensic astrology evidence in a criminal case?

No. Forensic astrology is not legal evidence and should not be treated as proof of guilt, innocence, cause of death, or motive. Vox Stella's forensic workflow is for symbolic event-chart analysis and research.

### Did Vox Stella identify the perpetrators in these cases?

No. The legal outcomes come from courts, police records, prosecutor records, and reputable reporting. Vox Stella was used after the fact to test whether a sourced event chart aligned with the known case pattern.

### Why did this article focus on only three Worst Roommate Ever cases?

The full inventory covered all nine released episodes. Only four had enough timing detail to run, and three aligned on the primary case pattern. The other cases were kept in the benchmark as holdouts or validation context.

### Why was the Dudley case included if survivability missed?

Because it aligned on the main case pattern: homicide, known housing relationship, and concealment. The survivability miss is important and should stay visible because it shows the limitation of using a probable sequence anchor instead of an exact event time.

### Why not use birth charts?

The benchmark uses event charts only. Birth charts require reliable birth data, especially birth time, and those data were not needed for this validation.

### Can I run other true-crime cases in Vox Stella?

Yes, but the best practice is to use only sourced event times. If you do not have a reliable time, do not invent one. Treat broad windows as research notes, not as clean event charts.
