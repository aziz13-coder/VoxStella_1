### INTRODUCTION

by Anthony Louis

Mundane astrology (from the Latin mundus meaning world, universe, humankind) is the branch of the celestial science dealing with political and world events. Heads of nations have consulted mundane astrologers for thousands of years. This form of astrology, for example, was a key factor in Alexander the Great's defeat of the Persian Empire's army at Gaugamela in 331 BC.

In more recent times the German astrologer Elsbeth Ebertin accurately predicted Hitler's political destiny in 1923, albeit from an allegedly incorrect horoscope cast for the Sun in the 29 th degree of Aries. [1] Hitler's natal Sun, in fact, occupied the 1 st degree of Taurus when he was born on 20 April 1899. Ebertin must have erroneously consulted the ephemeris for 19 April 1899, the day before Hitler's birth. As often happens in astrological forecasting, Ebertin's chart for Hitler "worked" quite well, even though it was not based on historically accurate information.

### Elsbeth

Ebertin died in Freiburg at the end of November 1944. Her son Reinhold later claimed that his mother had foreseen her own death but didn't want to relocate out of fear that her abrupt departure would panic her neighbors who believed she could tell the future. She chose to die in an air raid rather than upset her neighbors. What an altruistic lady she was!

Mundane astrology can be dangerous to practice. Throughout history, court astrologers have been executed for displeasing the ruler of the nation with their predictions, regardless of whether the forecast was accurate or not. In May of 1941 Reich Minister and amateur astrologer Rudolf Hess decided to fly to Scotland because he and his astrologers believed the stars foretold defeat for Germany. Hess wanted to negotiate a peace deal with the Allies. In response, Hitler became enraged and had the Gestapo round up hundreds of astrologers and occultists. [2] Several stargazers were executed and the rest sent to concentration camps. The Führer blamed astrology for Hess' betrayal of Hitler's megalomaniacal idea that the German master race should rule the world.

The book you are about to read looks at political astrology as it was practiced at the beginning of the 20 th century. Using Raphael's 1910 text on mundane astrology as a scaffold, I have revised and amply illustrated his original text with copious explanations, clarifications, case examples and modern charts. The Appendices contain a wealth of supplemental information that fleshes out Raphael's 80-page booklet, now in the public domain, and adds historical perspective to his approach.

During the last two centuries several authors have used the pen name "Raphael" to write books about astrology. They apparently chose the pseudonym because the Archangel Raphael is associated with Mercury – a god of magic, divination, astrology and writing. At the time this text was originally published in 1910, astrologers in the UK wrote under pseudonyms to avoid being thrown in jail for practicing the celestial art.

To verify which of the many Raphaels wrote the 1910 version of the current text, I contacted David Roell of the Astrology Center of America. David kindly responded with an email in which he explained that Raphael was the pseudonym of a series of men, starting with the first and most famous, Robert Cross Smith (1795 - 1832). Cross Smith, the original user of the Raphael pseudonym, was succeeded by John Palmer (1807-1837), and then by some lesser known writers (Dixon, Medhurst, Wakeley, Sparkes) and finally by Robert T. Cross (1850 – 1923), the well-known author of the text you are about to read.

In an article at skyscript.co.uk, Kim Farnell tells us that this Raphael was born at Brockley Farm, Worstead (East Anglia), England on 15 May 1850 at 2:35 in the morning. [3] His birth name was Frederick Robert Tuck Cross. Frederick Robert was the son of Thomas Robert Cross and Charlotte Rump. He later dropped the Frederick, perhaps to identify himself more closely with the first Raphael, Robert Cross Smith, and to capitalize on the original Raphael's fame. Having developed a strong interest in astrology at an early age, Robert T. Cross acquired the copyright to the well-known

### Raphael's Ephemeris

in the 1870s. During the period 1877-1879 Mr. Cross published a Guide to Astrology , which became quite popular. Robert T. Cross died on May 29, 1923 in Worstead, Norfolk. [4]

Robert Tuck Cross was a prolific writer with a knack for explaining complex astrological ideas in simple and direct language. He appears to have relied heavily on two 17 th century British texts for his information about mundane astrology: William Lilly's

### Christian Astrology

(1647) and William Ramesey's

### Astrology Restored

(1653). Parts of his 1910 text are copied verbatim from earlier sources without acknowledgement. Because Raphael's sources date back several centuries, some of his comments appear strange and antiquated to the modern reader, especially in his chapters on interpreting eclipses in the thirty-six decanates and assigning astrological rulers to various locations on earth. Nonetheless, the bulk of what Raphael presents in 1910 contains valuable information and is responsible to sparking and maintaining interest in mundane astrology for the past hundred years.

In editing and annotating Raphael's 1910 text, I have left the original text intact except for a few minor changes in spelling, formatting and punctuation. Errors and typos in the original have been corrected. Astrological data has been recalculated and rendered more accurate with the use of a modern computer. Any additions to the text appear in brackets. For example, my editorial comments, which are not part of the 1910 text, appear in brackets [ ] throughout this text.

I have added numerous astrological charts and case examples, not found in the original text, to illustrate Raphael's ideas and demonstrate how mundane astrology was practiced in the early 20 th century. My goal has been to present a version of Raphael's

### Mundane Astrology

that is readily accessible to the modern reader. The material in the

### Appendices

is not part of Raphael's original text. I have added extensive notes in the

### Appendices

to flesh out Raphael's ideas with historical case examples.

Raphael (1910) and H.S. Green (1911) each published brief texts that outlined the traditional methods of mundane astrology. There is much overlap between the two authors. In 1951, the renowned astrologer Charles Carter wrote a book which was critical of the classical methods summarized by Raphael and Green. Carter called their techniques "completely inadequate." There was method in Carter's madness. He had made a fool of himself by falsely predicting that England would not enter into war with Germany in 1939, and he blamed his inaccurate prognostication on the methods he learned from studying H.S. Green. In addition, Carter was dismissive of Alan Leo's work and referred to Leo's ventures into political forecasting as "feeble and unsuccessful," despite Leo's accurate prediction of the Second Boer War ten months before it occurred.

Carter was particul arly bothered by the importance that traditional astrologers placed on the cardinal ingresses of the Sun and the lunations (New and Full Moons) throughout the astrological year. He concluded from his studies of numerous mundane charts during World War II that solar cardinal ingresses and lunation charts, if studied in isolation, were barely worth casting. In Carter's experience these charts only appeared to have value when a planet or planets fell within one (or at most two) degrees of an angle. Carter may have been making a straw man of traditional astrologers because of the public ridicule he experienced after his false prediction. The 17 th century French astrologer Morinus, for example, never interpreted charts in isolation, but always considered a whole series of relevant charts before making a prediction.

Carter stresses the significance of the birth charts of the heads of state and other key figures of a nation. Comparing the nativities of the movers and shakers of a nation with the standard mundane charts (ingresses, New Moons, eclipses, major conjunctions, etc.) is necessary to flesh out and clarify the outline of the year shown by the ingresses.

An other important point made by Carter (1951), which barely gets addressed by Raphael or Green, is the use of "birth charts" of nations. These early 20 th century authors claim that nations are "ruled" by various zodiac signs or planets but rarely seem to use a specific chart of origin of a nation in their work. Carter stresses that if it were possible to ascertain a reliable initiation chart or "nativity" of a nation, then one could study such a chart and apply the transits and progressions to make accurate forecasts. Carter calls the traditional reliance on ingresses and lunations, when interpreted as stand-alone charts, "pitifully insufficient." I suspect most traditional astrologers would agree with him.

The reader should keep Carter's perspective in mind when going through Raphael's text. Much has happened in astrology since it was first published in 1910. Contemporary astrologers continue to contribute to our understanding of mundane charts with ideas such as midpoints, 90-degree dials, local space astrology, inception charts of cities and states, astro-cartography and the like. Nonetheless, this book by Raphael is well worth reading because it sets the stage for later developments and is one of the classics of modern mundane astrology.

### Anthony Louis

ASTROLOGY is that branch of the Science which treats of the influence of the Zodiac and Planets upon Nations, countries, cities and divisions of the earth, and is the art of foretelling the chief events which will come to pass therein.

A figure of the heavens is erected for certain times – to be afterwards explained – and for the particular places for which the forecasts are required, and the longitudes and declinations of the planets computed therefor, exactly in the same manner as in the erection of an ordinary horoscope.

As in Natal Astrology, different influences are ascribed to the planets and the twelve divisions of the heavens, and to make forecasts from the various figures of the heavens is not a very difficult task.

The student should bear in mind the various testimonies of good and evil shown, and thus may read the good or evil fortune of any particular nation or kingdom, the possibility of peace or war, health or sickness, dearth or plenty, in any particular part of the world for which the forecasts are required.

The student should bear in mind that all these aspects are of different strengths. The conjunction and the opposition are the most powerful, then the parallel , followed by the square , trine and sextile of about equal power, then the semi-square and sesquiquadrate .

The orbs of the aspects, or the number of degrees from an exact aspect, in which a planet will act upon another, requires very careful handling in Mundane Astrology, as only very close aspects should be considered .

The orbs of the planets Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune when forming the major aspects , conjunction, sextile, square, trine and opposition, may be reckoned as 5°. They are to be measured thus. Suppose Venus is forming a conjunction of Mars, then when Venus gets to within five degrees of Mars, the aspect will begin to operate, and will last until Venus is five degrees past it. The same with the other planets. The Sun and Moon have larger orbs. Eight degrees may be allowed the Sun and Moon.

For the minor aspects , smaller orbs should be allowed. For the Planets, 2° and for the Sun and Moon, 3°. It is a very difficult subject to deal with, but the above maybe taken as practically correct.

For the parallel , one degree either way should be allowed for the Sun, Moon and Planets. Thus, if the Sun is in 12° north declination, and Jupiter in 13° north, then they are in parallel and will remain in this aspect until the difference of their declinations amounts to more than a degree.

[Editor's Note : Raphael's example is confusing. He probably means that if two planets are in the same degree of declination, they are always in parallel. If the planets are in adjacent degrees of declination, as in the example with the Sun in 12° N and Jupiter in 13° N, then the Sun and Jupiter can be considered parallel as long as they are no more than 1° apart. If the Sun were at the beginning of 12° N and Jupiter at the end of 13° N, then they would be almost two degrees apart and not considered parallel.]

For the cusps of the mundane houses , observe the following rule. Find the number of degrees between one house and the next and divide it by three, and reckon this one third as the orb of the cusp of the house. Thus if the twelfth house is Aquarius 13°, and the Ascendant Aries 0°, find the difference between these two degrees, which is 47°, and one third of this is nearly 16°, so when a planet gets within 16 degrees of Aries 0°, it is to be reckoned as in the Ascendant, or first house.

[Editor's Note : This is an unusual rule, and I don't know Raphael's source for it. More commonly, astrologers have considered a planet in the last five degrees of a house to belong to the following house. Raphael's rule means that a planet in the final third of a house can be regarded as belonging in the subsequent house.]

[An alternative to the 5-degree rule or to Raphael's method of trisecting houses would be to use whole sign houses in which the sign of the ASC becomes the entire 1 st house, and the remaining signs in order become the remaining houses. While using house systems such as Placidus, I have found that a planet entering a sign often "triggers" an event related to the house with that sign on its cusp.]

CHAPTER VII: How to Erect the Mundane Maps for Foreign Parts.

As the subject of Mundane Astrology is principally intended for more advanced students, it is not intended to go into the matter of casting horoscopes and placing the planets therein, as these rules can be learned from the "Guide to Astrology," or the "Key."

### Once

the student can erect a map of the heavens for Greenwich the process of computing a figure for foreign countries is a very simple process, and it is particularly with this in view that the following simple rules and instructions are given.

Now as it is usually the practice, when making forecasts for the different countries of the world, to first cast the Mundane map for Greenwich and compute the planets' places for this particular time, it follows that all other maps required for places other than Greenwich can be found by merely ad ding or subtracting the difference in time between Greenwich and the place required.

The rule is a very simple one and merely consists of finding the longitude of the place required, and turning it into time at the rate of one hour for every fifteen degrees, or four minutes for every degree.

If the place is in West longitude subtract this time from the Right Ascension of the Meridian at the Greenwich Map, and if East add the time. The result will give the Right Ascension of the Meridian for the place required.

### Then

with this Right Ascension, and a Table of Houses for the latitude of the place, erect the Mundane map in the same way as you would an ordinary horoscope.

The planets' places will be the same in every map. That is to say, whatever the planetary longitudes and declinations are in the Mundane map for Greenwich, they will be exactly the same for all parts of the world.

As two illustrations of this simple method of erecting the Mundane maps for different parts of the world, we will take the Sun's ingress into Aries for March 21 st , 1911.

### Solar

and Lunar eclipses are prominent features in Mundane Astrology, for they indicate most important and far-reaching events, and their period of influence extends over some considerable time.

The figure for each solar eclipse should be erected for the time of the ecliptic conjunction of the luminaries, and that for each lunar eclipse, for the ecliptic opposition, and not for the time of central eclipse. The times of the ecliptic conjunction and opposition are those of the New and Full Moon.

### Eclipses

have most effect in those countries where they are visible , and more especially where they are on the meridian at the moment of central eclipse. They also affect the countries and cities ruled by the signs in which they are placed.

In eclipses of the Moon, it has been found that the effects commence almost immediately, but in those of the Sun it has been generally observed that some months elapse before the greatest effects appear, and also that the greater or lesser effects of each eclipse will be in proportion to the magnitude of such eclipse, or, in other words, the more total the eclipse the more powerfully will it operate upon the earth and its inhabitants. Also, that eclipses which are invisible are found to have no peculiar or perceptible influence over that region, city, or country in which they are invisible.

The united observations of Astrologers in every age and every clime confirm this opinion, and the student himself can easily prove it.

[Editor's Note : Raphael fails to mention that Babylonian astrologers paid special attention to planets that became visible and prominent during an eclipse. See Appendix VI for an example.]

In

### Ptolemy's Tetrabiblos

, we find the following rules laid down in regard to eclipses.

"In all eclipses of the Sun and Moon and especially in such as are fully visible, the place in the Zodiac where the eclipse happens is to be noted; and it must be seen what countries are in familiarity with that place… and, in like manner, it must be observed what cities are under the influence of the sign in which the eclipse happens, either by means of the Ascendant and the situation of the Luminaries at the time of their foundation…. Whatever countries or cities shall be thus found in familiarity with the ecliptical place will all be comprehended in the event, which will, however, principally attach to all those parts which may be connected with the identical sign of the eclipse, and in which it may be visible while above the earth."

### Further

it has been found that the effects of eclipses falling in the Fixed signs (Taurus, Leo, Scorpio, Aquarius) will have a very lasting effect; those that are formed in Cardinal signs (Aries, Cancer, Libra, Capricorn) will be brief and soon over, while those that are formed in Common signs (Gemini, Virgo, Sagittarius, Pisces) will commence sooner, and last longer, but that their effects will be liable to interruption, that is to say, they will continue, for a time, suddenly cease, and then commence again.

Now as regards the effects of eclipses, the sign position is first to be taken into account.

1 . Eclipses in Fiery signs. These threaten the destruction of cattle and sheep, exile or imprisonment or murder of some king or notable person, or great ruler. Much discontent and dissension among the people. Movements of armies, fighting, fires, fevers, pestilence, and scarcity of the fruits of the earth, especially in those regions affected by the eclipse.

2 . Eclipses in Earthy signs. These foreshadow a scarcity of corn and products of the earth by drought, and cause earthquakes, mining disasters, and great agricultural depression.

3 . Eclipses in Airy signs. Famine, sickness, pestilence, and tempests and stormy winds hurtful to mankind.

4 . Eclipses in Watery signs. These denote much mortality among the common people, great destruction of fowls and fishes, and such things as live in or near the sea.

The strength of the planet ruling the sign in which the eclipse falls should be considered, and its position in the figure of such eclipse duly noted, for the significations of this planet will principally appear.

Thus, if Mars rules over the sign in which the eclipse falls, and is placed in the eighth house of the figure, it will denote some grievous calamity, causing many sudden and terrible deaths: if in the sixth house much sickness, if in the third many terrible railway accidents. The same with the other malefics.

When the benefics rule over the place of the eclipse, the effects are better, but much depends upon the strength of the ruling planet.

The effects of eclipses are spread over a long period in proportion to the duration of the eclipse. If a solar eclipse, then the effects will last as many years as the eclipse is hours in duration. Thus the total eclipse of November 1 st , 1910, lasts 4h. 35m., equal to four years seven months.

If a lunar eclipse, then the effects will last for as many months as the eclipse is hours long. The lunar eclipse of November 16 th , 1910, lasts for 3h. 14m., equal to three months seven days. It is not altogether certain whether this period of duration means from the date of the eclipse, or from the commencement of its effects, but it is more probably the latter.

Several rules are given in regard to this matter, and experience alone can best decide which are correct.

### Cardan

says, "To know when the effects of an eclipse will begin to be felt, take the distance of the rising of the luminary to the middle of the eclipse, or from the middle of the eclipse to the next rising of the luminary in hours and minutes. The proportion of time the length of day may bear to the year in the case of a Solar eclipse, and the proportion of time the night may bear to the year in a Lunar eclipse, will show the proportion of the year due to the interval obtained."

### Ptolemy says

1 . If the eclipse falls in the eastern horizon, the effects will manifest themselves about the next four months, and most strongly will operate during the first third of such period.

2 . If it falls in the Midheaven, the events thereof will begin to appear from the fourth to the eighth month following the eclipse, and the chief effects will happen during the second or middle part of that period.

3 . If falling in the western horizon, the effects will appear from the eighth to the twelfth month, following the eclipse, and the chief effects will be felt in the last part of that period.

The most reliable rule, as far as experience goes, is to calculate the time of Sunrise or Sunset from the middle of the eclipse, and reckon this time at the rate of one day for every four minutes, or 24 hours to the year.

In the next two chapters are given the effects of solar and lunar eclipses when falling in the three decanates of each sign. These observations have been handed down to us, and have been found to be very reliable, if not entirely so.

It should be observed here that a decanate is the third part of a sign, and consists of ten degrees. The first decanate of a sign extends from 0° to 10°, the second from 10° to 20°, and the third from 20° to the end of the sign.

APPENDIX VI: The Bloody Lunar Eclipse of the Battle of Gaugamela

The great battle of Gaugamela on October 1 st of 331 BC, marked the end of the Persia Empire under Darius III and the beginning of the Hellenistic period. Alexander's victory over Darius III is remarkable because the Persian army vastly outnumbered Alexander's forces. It is fair to say that the lunar eclipse of September 20 th tipped the balance in favor of Alexander.

Alexander the Great had been in pursuit of the Persian army and received information that Darius and his army could be found across the Tigris River. Shortly after Alexander crossed the Tigris, there was a total eclipse of the moon, which was said to have turned blood red, freaking out Alexander's troops who regarded eclipses as evil omens. Ever a consummate politician, Alexander told his men that the eclipse was indeed an evil omen but for Darius and his army who would soon be defeated by the Macedonian forces. The reddened moon, claimed Alexander, referred to the spilled blood of the Persians.

The Roman historian Pliny later wrote that the eclipse occurred in the second hour of the night. Using a modern computer, we are able to reconstruct the chart for the eclipse, cast for 9:12:36 pm LMT (Full Moon phase) in the area of Gaugamela. According to some sources, Gaugamela was located about 100 kilometers (62 miles) west of the modern city of Erbil, Iraq (coordinates: 36°11′28″ N, 44°00′33″ E). Doing the trigonometry [28] to determine the coordinates of the point 100 km due west of Erbil, I estimated the coordinates of Gaugamela to be approximately 36° 11′ N, 42° 55' E.

(A different source [29] estimates the coordinates of Gaugamela to be 36° 34′ N, 43° 29' E. These coordinates would only slightly change the ASC of the eclipse chart presented below to 1° 42′ Gemini and the MC to 10° 41′ Aquarius. In any case, we do not know the exact location of either Alexander or Darius III on 20 September 331 BC, but we do know that they were both in the vicinity of these coordinates and thus the chart of the eclipse would have roughly 1° Gemini rising and 10° Aquarius on the Midheaven.)

On 20 September 331 BC, the sun set in Gaugamela at 6:04 pm LMT, and each hour of the night on that date was 59 minutes long. The second hour of the night mentioned by Pliny began at 7:03 pm. Jupiter was faintly visible at the western horizon until it set at 8:04 pm. The Full Moon stage of the eclipse occurred at 9:13 pm. Mercury, Venus, Mars and the sun would not have been visible, as they were all below the horizon. Saturn was less than eight degrees away from the Moon in Pisces, and astrologers of the time would have interpreted the eclipse as occurring in conjunction with Saturn.

With the Moon totally eclipsed, Saturn is the only classical planet visible above the horizon and in total control of the night sky. Good news for Alexander, bad news for Darius III.

The tablets mention that the total phase of the eclipse lasted 40 minutes, but modern calculations set the duration of totality at 63 minutes.

The Persian Empire was well known for its astrologers, and no doubt King Darius III was provided with a delineation of the eclipse by his astrologers. It would not have been a favorable report. For centuries the astrologer-astronomers at the Marduk temple Esagila of Babylonia (coordinates: 32° 32′ 02" N, 44° 25' 17" E) recorded on cuneiform clay tablets their famous star catalogues detailing the movements of stars in the heavens and making correlations with happenings on earth. Eclipses were regularly recorded, starting in the 8 th century BC. In the Almagest, Ptolemy writes that he had access to eclipse records dating back to 747 BC. Astrology in Babylonia was highly empirical and did not rely on the type of mathematical theorizing added by the Greeks of antiquity.

Over many years Mesopotamian astrologers had noticed several variables which they correlated with the effect of an eclipse. These variables included the area of the moon that was eclipsed, the compass direction that the eclipse shadow was moving across the moon, which way the wind was blowing during the eclipse, the appearance and prominence of any planets in the sky at the time of the eclipse and the relative absence of planets which could only be faintly seen or had set below the horizon. The surface of the moon was divided into four quadrants which were correlated with the four quarters of the earth surrounding the observer's position.

The Babylonian star records recorded and interpreted a total lunar eclipse on 22 May 678 BC (7:01:12 pm LMT, Esagila). This eclipse occurred with the moon in the 23 rd degree of Scorpio, just above the eastern horizon. Jupiter in the 28 th degree of Cancer was clearly visible as the most elevated planet in the night sky. Shortly before the eclipse, Venus had set at the location of the Marduk temple. Mars was below the horizon near the IC. Mercury and Saturn were close to the western horizon and about to set. The sun had set at about 6:41 pm LMT, about twenty minutes before the Full Moon stage of the eclipse.

The interpretation of this 678 BC eclipse by the 7 th century BC astrologer Munnabitu for the Neo-Assyrian king has been preserved. The report details which part of the moon was first eclipsed and in what manner the eclipse cleared the moon. These regions are paired with directions and locations on earth where the effect of the eclipse will be felt. Because the shadow of the total eclipse moved from northeast to southwest across the moon, the lands to the south and west of Esagila would feel its evil influence. Munnabitu adds, however, that because the eclipse is total, it serves as a sign for all lands. Furthermore, Jupiter being elevated and prominent at the time of the eclipse was a sure sign of well-being for the king. The astrologers advising King Darius would have been well aware of Munnabitu's analysis of this eclipse.

If a clearly visible Jupiter during the total lunar eclipse of 678 BC proved favorable for the king, what would be the meaning of an invisible Jupiter and a highly prominent Saturn during the 331 BC eclipse? Presumably with the Moon, the queen of the night, totally eclipsed, the sky belonged entirely to Saturn to perform his usual mischief. Babylonian astrologers had long observed that a lunar eclipse while Jupiter was invisible was ominous for the reigning king, and a prominent Saturn was also a bad sign. Not good news for King Darius!

APPENDIX IX: Evangeline Adams, Symbolic Charts and the Inception of the United States

As a teenager just getting into astrology in the late 1950s, I found few books on astrology in my local small town library. One of them was Astrology for Everyone (1931) by Evangeline Adams (1868-1932). In the introduction to her book, Adams said that she used a chart for the United States cast for 3:03 a.m. on July 4 th , 1776 in Philadelphia. On the basis of this chart Adams predicted in 1931 that the United States would enter into war in 1942 when Uranus entered Gemini for the first time since 1860 at the time of the American Civil War.

Adams died a year after she made her famous prediction. I was extremely impressed by her forecast but wondered how she arrived at a birth time of 3:03 a.m. for the United States of America. Adams believed that the signers of the Declaration reached a positive decision to declare independence at that moment, but it seemed to me that the delegates to the Continental Congress would have been asleep in their beds 3:03 in the morning.

Years later , when I learned about the importance of eclipses in mundane astrology, it occurred to me that whoever came up with the 3:03 a.m. USA chart may have been utilizing an eclipse of the summer of 1776 to generate the cusps for the chart. It turns out that there were two Solar Partial Eclipses that summer, one on 15 July 1776 and the other on 14 August 1776.

Although July 4 th is the day the United States celebrates the Declaration of Independence, there were several other important dates related to declaring independence from Britain in the summer of 1776. Two days earlier, on 2 July 1776, the Continental Congress voted to adopt a motion proposed by Virginia to separate from Britain. On July 4 th the Declaration of Independence was formally adopted by twelve colonies.

### New York

It was not until August 2 nd , 1776 that the engrossed copy was signed by most of the delegates with the exception of Elbridge Gerry, Oliver Wolcott, Lewis Morris, Thomas McKean and Matthew Thornton who signed at a later date. The Solar Eclipse closest to the August 2 nd signing of the officially inscribed copy of the Declaration of Independence was the Solar Partial Eclipse of 14 August 1776. The angles of this eclipse at Philadelphia are the same as the ones found in the Adams 3:03 a.m. chart.

There are two methods commonly employed to cast an eclipse chart. The one favored by Raphael was to use the time of the exact lunar phase, that is, the exact moment of the New or Full Moon at the time of the eclipse. The second method, and the one apparently preferred by Adams, is to use the exact moment of maximum eclipse, defined as the instant when the Moon comes closest to the axis of Earth's shadow, or when the Moon's shadow passes closest to the center of the Earth. The exact lunar phase and the moment of maximum eclipse can differ by up to about 15 minutes or about 4 degrees of R.A. on the MC.
